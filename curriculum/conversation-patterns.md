# Recognising Conversation Patterns: the Nobody's Simple field syllabus

**Editorial status:** Curriculum map, not yet a collection of published lessons. **Entries:** 209. **Audience:** everyday learners, including people trying to understand difficult relationships, workplace conflicts and online disputes.

## How to use this syllabus
These are **behaviour patterns, descriptive concepts and reasoning errors—not 209 diagnostic signs of narcissistic personality disorder**. Some names are established constructs (such as DARVO, gaslighting and standard logical fallacies); others are useful editorial descriptions for teaching recognition, not validated psychological scales. A person can behave defensively, argue badly or even manipulate someone without meeting criteria for any personality disorder. Coercive control is better understood as a *pattern* of power, constraint and harm, not an isolated phrase.

For **each** technique, make a separate lesson that teaches: (1) what the learner directly observes, (2) several manifestations in different settings, (3) a plausible non-abusive or alternative explanation, (4) which further evidence distinguishes them, (5) a proportionate response or safe limit, and (6) spaced practice. Avoid labels that pretend to read a speaker's motives. Do not reward users simply for choosing "manipulation"; a key skill is identifying when evidence is insufficient.

The example quotations below are **fictional instructional examples**. They are not proof of any speaker's diagnosis or intent.

## 1. Reality, memory and credibility (16)

| Technique or reasoning error | What to notice | Fictional example |
|---|---|---|
| **Gaslighting** | Repeatedly undermining a person's confidence in what they saw or remember | “That never happened; you invent things.” |
| **Memory rewriting** | Replacing a shared account with a self-serving version | “I actually warned you weeks ago.” |
| **Evidence denial** | Rejecting verifiable information without engaging it | “That recording proves nothing.” |
| **Reality inversion** | Claiming the opposite of an observable action | “I wasn't shouting; you were.” |
| **Moving the factual baseline** | Altering which facts count as agreed | “We never agreed that was the deadline.” |
| **Selective omission** | Leaving out details that substantially change the account | “You only heard the end of the call.” |
| **Half-truth** | Giving accurate fragments that mislead when combined | “I told them you were late, but not why.” |
| **False certainty** | Speaking more confidently than the evidence warrants | “Everyone knows exactly what you meant.” |
| **Confusion flooding** | Introducing incompatible assertions faster than they can be checked | “The email is forged, but I never sent it anyway.” |
| **Discrediting memory** | Treating ordinary uncertainty as proof of incompetence | “You forgot one detail, so none of this counts.” |
| **Denial of witnessed emotion** | Dismissing an expressed feeling as fabricated | “You aren't hurt; you're performing.” |
| **Credibility erosion** | Repeatedly portraying someone as unreliable to others | “She's always confused about these things.” |
| **Manufactured contradiction** | Rephrasing an earlier statement to make a person appear inconsistent | “Yesterday you said you wanted freedom, so why complain?” |
| **Context stripping** | Quoting a sentence without its important surrounding context | “You said 'I don't care'—end of discussion.” |
| **Document or screenshot cherry-picking** | Showing a selective record while hiding contrary material | “Here is the one message where you agreed.” |
| **Forced reinterpretation** | Insisting the recipient adopt one account of their own experience | “What you really felt was jealousy.” |

## 2. Accountability diversion and reversal (16)

| Technique or reasoning error | What to notice | Fictional example |
|---|---|---|
| **DARVO** | Deny wrongdoing, attack the challenger, then portray oneself as the harmed party | “I did nothing; you're cruel for accusing me.” |
| **Blame shifting** | Moving responsibility for a decision onto the person affected | “I only lied because you ask questions.” |
| **Victim blaming** | Treating harm as deserved or caused by the target's harmless behaviour | “You should have known better than to upset me.” |
| **Provocation excuse** | Using another person's behaviour to erase one's own agency | “You made me swear at you.” |
| **Whataboutism** | Changing the subject to a different alleged mistake | “But what about the time you forgot my birthday?” |
| **Tu quoque** | Rejecting criticism by alleging the critic also does it | “You do it too, so you can't object.” |
| **Minimisation** | Reducing a meaningful harm to something supposedly trivial | “It was only a joke.” |
| **Rationalisation** | Offering a convenient justification after the fact | “I hid it to protect you.” |
| **Pseudo-apology** | Using apology language without accepting the action | “I'm sorry you chose to feel offended.” |
| **Conditional apology** | Making repair depend on the target withdrawing their claim | “I'll apologise when you stop accusing me.” |
| **Apology bargaining** | Demanding instant forgiveness as payment for saying sorry | “I apologised, so you owe me normality.” |
| **Intent-over-impact defence** | Treating lack of harmful intent as cancellation of consequences | “I didn't mean it, so there's no issue.” |
| **Obligation reversal** | Demanding comfort from the person who raised the harm | “Now you need to reassure me I'm not terrible.” |
| **Moral bookkeeping** | Using unrelated past good deeds to cancel responsibility | “After everything I've done, this one mistake doesn't count.” |
| **Counter-accusation cascade** | Answering a specific concern with several unrelated accusations | “You lie, interrupt and never appreciate me.” |
| **Responsibility diffusion** | Claiming many people caused a decision to avoid personal accountability | “We all thought it was fine, so it's nobody's fault.” |

## 3. Shame, contempt and status pressure (16)

| Technique or reasoning error | What to notice | Fictional example |
|---|---|---|
| **Personal attack** | Substituting criticism of a person for evaluation of the issue | “You're too immature to understand.” |
| **Humiliation** | Exposing a person's vulnerability to gain social power | “Tell everyone how you cried.” |
| **Belittling** | Regularly speaking to reduce another person's confidence | “Aw, you tried to think.” |
| **Infantilisation** | Treating an adult as incapable of normal judgement | “Let the grown-ups decide.” |
| **Condescension** | Using patronising explanation as a status signal | “I'll use little words for you.” |
| **Contempt signalling** | Communicating disgust or worthlessness instead of disagreement | “You're embarrassing to be around.” |
| **Mocking vulnerability** | Using a disclosed insecurity as ammunition | “There goes your abandonment issue again.” |
| **Shame leverage** | Making compliance a test of whether someone is decent | “A good daughter would agree.” |
| **Status comparison** | Making a request seem necessary to avoid looking inferior | “Other partners don't make this difficult.” |
| **Superiority claim** | Presenting oneself as above basic accountability | “People at my level don't owe explanations.” |
| **Character assassination** | Drawing broad conclusions about personality from one act | “You forgot once, so you're selfish.” |
| **Competence undermining** | Questioning someone's general competence after a specific disagreement | “If you can't follow this, you can't manage anything.” |
| **Public correction theatre** | Using an audience to convert correction into humiliation | “Let's explain your mistake in front of everyone.” |
| **Sarcasm as cover** | Delivering contempt then claiming humour cancels it | “Oh, genius idea—I'm only joking.” |
| **Diagnostic labelling as a weapon** | Using a mental-health label as a verdict without assessment | “You're a narcissist, so nothing you say matters.” |
| **Therapy-speak weaponisation** | Borrowing clinical language to avoid discussing a specific act | “Your boundary is just your anxious attachment.” |

## 4. Affection, attachment and guilt leverage (16)

| Technique or reasoning error | What to notice | Fictional example |
|---|---|---|
| **Love bombing** | Overwhelming idealisation or attention used in a wider control pattern | “You're my soulmate; quit your job and move tomorrow.” |
| **Future faking** | Making repeated promises of future change without corresponding steps | “After the wedding everything will be different.” |
| **Intermittent reinforcement** | Alternating warmth and distance in ways that can intensify pursuit | “I'm affectionate only after you give in.” |
| **Conditional affection** | Offering closeness only in return for compliance | “Be agreeable and I'll cuddle you.” |
| **Affection withdrawal** | Withholding warmth as a punishment rather than communicating a need | “No hugs until you admit I'm right.” |
| **Silent-treatment punishment** | Withdrawing contact to penalise or control rather than taking an agreed pause | “I'll ignore you until you learn.” |
| **Guilt-tripping** | Using guilt rather than reasons to secure an action | “After all I've sacrificed, you can't say no.” |
| **Pity leverage** | Using a sad story to make refusal morally costly | “You know how lonely I am; don't leave tonight.” |
| **Abandonment threat** | Threatening to end the relationship to win a particular argument | “Agree right now or we're finished.” |
| **Jealousy induction** | Provoking insecurity to elicit pursuit or compliance | “Maybe someone else would value me.” |
| **Comparison with ex-partners** | Using a previous partner as leverage in current conflict | “My ex never argued about this.” |
| **Soulmate exceptionalism** | Claiming a unique bond exempts the relationship from ordinary boundaries | “If we're meant to be, we don't need privacy.” |
| **Rescue dependency** | Positioning oneself as the only person able to help | “Nobody else would put up with you.” |
| **Reciprocity trap** | Treating unsolicited generosity as a debt owed | “I bought you gifts; now you must agree.” |
| **Forgiveness coercion** | Demanding the other person stop having feelings after an apology | “If you loved me you'd already be over it.” |
| **Emotional hostage-taking** | Making another person's distress entirely dependent on compliance | “If you leave, my breakdown will be your fault.” |

## 5. Boundaries, threats and coercive control (17)

| Technique or reasoning error | What to notice | Fictional example |
|---|---|---|
| **Coercive ultimatum** | Attaching a disproportionate penalty to an ordinary choice | “Let me read your phone or we're done.” |
| **Threat of exposure** | Using private information as bargaining power | “Do this or I'll post those screenshots.” |
| **Intimidation** | Making aggression or retaliation likely enough to silence disagreement | “Keep arguing and see what happens.” |
| **Boundary reversal** | Recasting control of someone else's choices as a personal boundary | “My boundary is that you cannot see your friends.” |
| **Demand for immediate decision** | Removing time needed to consider consequential choices | “Say yes this minute or lose your chance.” |
| **No-win double bind** | Setting incompatible expectations that make failure inevitable | “Be honest, but don't tell me anything upsetting.” |
| **Moving goalposts** | Changing success criteria after they have been met | “Now prove you really mean the apology.” |
| **Rule asymmetry** | Applying a rule to one person while exempting oneself | “Your friendships are inappropriate; mine are different.” |
| **Unilateral rule-making** | Treating preferences as binding agreements without negotiation | “I decided that you must be home by six.” |
| **Surveillance normalisation** | Framing constant monitoring as routine relationship care | “Couples who care share live location always.” |
| **Privacy invasion** | Requiring access to messages, devices or accounts | “Show me every DM to prove loyalty.” |
| **Financial gatekeeping** | Using money or access to resources to direct choices | “I pay rent, so you don't get an opinion.” |
| **Movement restriction** | Controlling where a person can go or who they see | “You're not going out without my approval.” |
| **Sexual pressure** | Treating relationship status or past intimacy as consent | “We're together, so you owe me tonight.” |
| **Threatened self-harm as leverage** | Using a threat of self-injury to control another person's decision | “If you break up with me, I'll hurt myself.” |
| **Coerced confession** | Pressing for admission despite an account being disputed | “Admit you meant to hurt me before we can talk.” |
| **Compliance testing** | Using small demands to test whether refusal will be punished | “If you love me, hand over the password.” |

## 6. Triangulation, reputation and isolation (15)

| Technique or reasoning error | What to notice | Fictional example |
|---|---|---|
| **Triangulation** | Bringing a third person into a conflict to shift influence or loyalty | “Even your sister thinks you're wrong.” |
| **Flying-monkey recruitment** | Enlisting others to pressure or attack someone on a dispute | “I've told all our friends to message you.” |
| **Smear campaign** | Spreading a damaging narrative to undermine credibility | “I've been warning people you're unstable.” |
| **Pre-emptive discrediting** | Undermining a person before they can give their account | “She'll twist the story if she contacts you.” |
| **Rumour laundering** | Repeating hearsay as if it were independent evidence | “Everyone has heard what you're like.” |
| **False consensus** | Claiming unanimous support without credible evidence | “Literally everyone is on my side.” |
| **Coalition pressure** | Using group loyalty to suppress disagreement | “The team all agreed; don't be difficult.” |
| **Divide and conquer** | Separating people to make them dependent on one central actor | “Don't tell them what I said about you.” |
| **Social isolation pressure** | Making contact with supporters emotionally costly | “Your friends are turning you against me.” |
| **Loyalty test** | Demanding proof of allegiance through unreasonable sacrifice | “If you're loyal you'll cut them off.” |
| **Public-private mismatch** | Presenting as considerate publicly while controlling privately | “I'll agree on stage; we'll discuss your punishment later.” |
| **Reputation blackmail** | Using someone's fear of being mischaracterised to obtain compliance | “I can make you look untrustworthy to your boss.” |
| **Gatekeeping information** | Controlling what each participant learns about others | “Only ask me about the changes; not the team.” |
| **Status-based silencing** | Claiming rank makes criticism illegitimate | “I'm the parent, so I cannot be questioned.” |
| **Institutional betrayal** | Using a process or authority to dismiss a well-founded complaint | “HR says the case is closed because he is important.” |

## 7. Conversational obstruction and topic control (17)

| Technique or reasoning error | What to notice | Fictional example |
|---|---|---|
| **Circular argument** | Returning endlessly to the premise after it has been addressed | “But why? That's still not an answer.” |
| **Word salad** | Combining loosely related claims until the central issue disappears | “The timing, your attitude, loyalty, and society prove my point.” |
| **Gish gallop** | Making many weak claims faster than they can be examined | “Here are fifteen reasons in one breath.” |
| **Topic switching** | Moving to a different subject when asked about a concrete concern | “Why aren't we talking about your spending?” |
| **Deflection by detail** | Fixating on a minor accuracy issue while avoiding the central harm | “It was 8:03, not 8:05.” |
| **Semantic nitpicking** | Substituting a dispute over wording for a relevant answer | “Define exactly what 'rude' means.” |
| **Exhaustion tactic** | Keeping conflict going until resistance weakens | “We aren't ending this conversation until you agree.” |
| **Interruption control** | Repeatedly preventing someone from completing their account | “Stop. No. Listen. Let me finish.” |
| **Question barrage** | Asking so many questions that the original request is displaced | “Who said? When? Why? How can you prove it all?” |
| **Loaded question** | Putting an unsupported allegation inside the question | “When did you stop lying to me?” |
| **Demand for impossible proof** | Requiring evidence that cannot reasonably exist | “Prove you never thought about leaving me.” |
| **Unfalsifiable accusation** | Making every possible response confirm the allegation | “Denying you're selfish is itself proof.” |
| **Catch-22 demand** | Making compliance dependent on a condition compliance cannot satisfy | “I'll trust you once you show you don't need trust.” |
| **Selective reply** | Answering only the least challenging part of a message | “I responded to your typo, not your concern.” |
| **Conversation monopolisation** | Taking nearly all the speaking time to prevent examination | “I'll talk for twenty minutes; you get thirty seconds.” |
| **Stonewalling** | Refusing to engage at all when repair and dialogue are possible | “I'm not discussing anything ever.” |
| **False closure** | Declaring a question settled without answering it | “We've dealt with this. End of story.” |

## 8. Projection and motive attribution (12)

| Technique or reasoning error | What to notice | Fictional example |
|---|---|---|
| **Projection** | Attributing to someone else an impulse or trait one may be struggling with oneself | “You're hiding things, says the person concealing the account.” |
| **Mind-reading claim** | Presenting an inference about someone's motive as established fact | “You asked because you want to embarrass me.” |
| **Hostile intent attribution** | Assuming malice from an ambiguous action | “You didn't reply because you wanted to punish me.” |
| **Motivation substitution** | Changing what the other person says they want into a less credible motive | “You say you need rest, but you want power.” |
| **Emotional invalidation** | Treating another person's feeling as illegitimate rather than discussing conduct | “You have no right to be upset.” |
| **Negative trait essentialism** | Making a temporary action into a permanent character judgment | “You're always that kind of person.” |
| **Identity entrapment** | Using a stated value as proof someone must comply | “You call yourself caring, so say yes.” |
| **Pathologising dissent** | Explaining disagreement away as a disorder | “Your objections are just paranoia.” |
| **Overinterpreting body language** | Claiming a gesture proves a hidden motive | “You looked away, so you must be lying.” |
| **Psychological omniscience** | Claiming special access to a person's inner state | “I know you better than you know yourself.” |
| **Splitting into all-good/all-bad** | Treating someone as wholly admirable or wholly worthless after change | “You were perfect yesterday; now you're nothing.” |
| **Projective identification claim** | Pushing a caricatured role onto someone then reacting as if it is established | “You are the angry one; why are you defending yourself?” |

## 9. Work, education and digital variants (14)

| Technique or reasoning error | What to notice | Fictional example |
|---|---|---|
| **Credit appropriation** | Claiming another person's contribution as one's own | “I built the model alone.” |
| **Authority laundering** | Using a boss or policy as a shield for a personal demand | “Management says you have to, though no policy exists.” |
| **Performance concern as camouflage** | Using vague performance language to dodge a specific grievance | “Let's discuss your attitude rather than the missing payment.” |
| **Moving deliverables** | Changing what counts as completion after work is submitted | “That report isn't enough; now do the dashboard.” |
| **Unequal documentation** | Recording one person's errors while disregarding equivalent behaviour | “We log your delays, not the manager's.” |
| **Public praise/private devaluation** | Using praise in front of others while undermining privately | “Brilliant in the meeting, useless in my office.” |
| **Deadline ambush** | Springing an impossible timeframe to force dependence | “It's due in an hour; only I can rescue it.” |
| **Selective CC escalation** | Adding authority figures to intimidate rather than resolve | “I've copied the director onto this minor dispute.” |
| **Digital monitoring** | Using online status or location data to interrogate | “You were active at 10:12. Explain.” |
| **Screenshot ambush** | Presenting isolated private messages without context | “Look at this cropped line you sent.” |
| **Algorithmic pile-on** | Recruiting online attention as pressure | “I'll post this so everyone can judge you.” |
| **Harassment through persistence** | Repeatedly contacting after a clear refusal | “I messaged on five platforms until you answered.” |
| **AI-assisted impersonation** | Generating plausible false messages or evidence | “This synthetic voice note proves your admission.” |
| **Reputational threat through platform control** | Threatening to misuse access to shared accounts or audiences | “I'll delete your posts if you complain.” |

## 10. Fallacies: relevance and diversion (18)

| Technique or reasoning error | What to notice | Fictional example |
|---|---|---|
| **Ad hominem** | Attacking the speaker instead of addressing their claim | “You failed school, so your evidence is wrong.” |
| **Abusive ad hominem** | Using direct insult as an argumentative substitute | “Only an idiot would say that.” |
| **Circumstantial ad hominem** | Dismissing a claim because of the speaker's situation | “You'd say that because you work there.” |
| **Poisoning the well** | Discrediting a person before they speak | “Whatever she tells you is a lie.” |
| **Straw man** | Replacing a position with a weaker version | “You want fewer calls, so you don't care about family.” |
| **Hollow man** | Inventing an opponent or view to defeat | “People are saying we should ban all friendship.” |
| **Red herring** | Introducing irrelevant material to redirect attention | “The rent is overdue, but look how messy you are.” |
| **Appeal to emotion** | Replacing evidence with a feeling as proof | “This makes me furious, so it's clearly unjust.” |
| **Appeal to pity** | Using sympathy as if it establishes a factual conclusion | “I've had a hard week, therefore I wasn't wrong.” |
| **Appeal to fear** | Making a frightening outcome substitute for justification | “Agree or everything could collapse.” |
| **Appeal to force** | Using threatened consequences as if they prove truth | “My decision is right because I can fire you.” |
| **Appeal to ridicule** | Making an opposing claim seem laughable without examination | “Imagine believing that.” |
| **Appeal to popularity** | Treating widespread belief as proof | “A million people agree, so I am right.” |
| **Appeal to tradition** | Assuming longevity proves correctness | “We've always done it this way.” |
| **Appeal to novelty** | Assuming newness proves superiority | “It's the newest method, so it must work.” |
| **Appeal to authority** | Using a named expert where relevant reasons remain missing | “A celebrity therapist said it, end of debate.” |
| **Appeal to celebrity** | Using fame rather than relevant expertise | “This actor agrees, so the diagnosis is obvious.” |
| **Appeal to nature** | Assuming natural or instinctive means morally justified | “Jealousy is natural, so controlling you is fine.” |

## 11. Fallacies: evidence and language (18)

| Technique or reasoning error | What to notice | Fictional example |
|---|---|---|
| **False dilemma** | Presenting two options where more exist | “Either give me your password or you are cheating.” |
| **False equivalence** | Treating meaningfully different actions as the same | “A joke and a threat are both just words.” |
| **Hasty generalisation** | Drawing a sweeping conclusion from too few cases | “Two friends cancelled, so nobody is loyal.” |
| **Cherry-picking** | Selecting favourable evidence while excluding important counterevidence | “These two messages prove my entire case.” |
| **Anecdotal fallacy** | Treating an anecdote as decisive over a broader dataset | “My cousin improved, so the treatment always works.” |
| **Texas sharpshooter** | Finding a pattern after searching data without acknowledging the search | “These four dates prove a perfect cycle.” |
| **Moving the burden of proof** | Demanding the other side disprove an unsupported assertion | “Prove you aren't secretly jealous.” |
| **Argument from ignorance** | Treating absence of disproof as proof | “Nobody disproved it, so it happened.” |
| **Circular reasoning** | Using the conclusion as its own premise | “I am trustworthy because I never lie, and I never lie because I'm trustworthy.” |
| **Begging the question** | Assuming the disputed point in the premises | “As a bad partner, you obviously caused this.” |
| **Equivocation** | Switching the meaning of a word mid-argument | “I said 'free' meaning available, not free of charge.” |
| **Ambiguity exploitation** | Using an unclear statement to preserve deniability | “I said I might help—whatever that meant.” |
| **No true Scotsman** | Redefining a group to dismiss counterexamples | “No real friend would disagree.” |
| **Special pleading** | Granting oneself an exception without a relevant reason | “The rule applies to everyone but me.” |
| **Shifting standards of evidence** | Applying demanding standards to the other side and weak ones to oneself | “Your screenshot needs verification; my rumour is enough.” |
| **Quote mining** | Using a short quotation stripped of material context | “You said 'I hate this' about that one task.” |
| **False precision** | Giving unwarranted numerical accuracy | “There's a 97 percent chance you're lying.” |
| **Conflation** | Treating distinct concepts as identical | “Privacy means secrecy, and secrecy means guilt.” |

## 12. Fallacies: cause, consequence and inference (20)

| Technique or reasoning error | What to notice | Fictional example |
|---|---|---|
| **Post hoc fallacy** | Assuming sequence establishes causation | “You got promoted after meeting me, so I caused it.” |
| **Correlation-is-causation** | Treating association as proof of mechanism | “You texted less and got sad, so texts caused it.” |
| **Single-cause fallacy** | Explaining a complex event using one factor without justification | “Everything failed because you were late.” |
| **Slippery slope** | Claiming a small act inevitably produces an extreme end | “If you go out once, you'll abandon me.” |
| **Faulty analogy** | Treating superficially similar situations as equivalent | “A workplace is a family, so you must obey.” |
| **Composition fallacy** | Assuming a part's property necessarily applies to the whole | “One colleague cheated, so the company is corrupt.” |
| **Division fallacy** | Assuming a whole's property necessarily applies to each part | “The team won, so every member excelled.” |
| **Affirming the consequent** | Inferring a cause just because a possible effect occurred | “If guilty you are nervous; you're nervous, so guilty.” |
| **Denying the antecedent** | Assuming one absent cause rules out an effect | “If you cared you'd call; you didn't call, so you don't care.” |
| **Non sequitur** | Drawing a conclusion that does not follow from the premises | “You were tired, so my demand was fair.” |
| **Argument from consequences** | Judging a claim true or false from whether belief is pleasant | “If that were true I'd be hurt, so it isn't true.” |
| **Appeal to inevitability** | Treating predicted outcomes as reasons to accept conduct | “Arguments are inevitable, so apologies are pointless.” |
| **Sunk-cost fallacy** | Treating past investment as a reason to continue harmful investment | “We've spent five years, so we must stay together.” |
| **Moral licensing** | Treating past virtue as permission for present misconduct | “I volunteered all month, so this lie doesn't matter.” |
| **Middle-ground fallacy** | Assuming the truth must sit halfway between opposing positions | “One says it happened, one denies it; half happened.” |
| **Base-rate neglect** | Ignoring the frequency of ordinary explanations | “A late reply must mean betrayal.” |
| **Survivorship bias** | Drawing conclusions only from cases that remain visible | “Every successful entrepreneur dropped out, so quit school.” |
| **Appeal to probability** | Treating possibility as actuality | “You could be hiding something, therefore you are.” |
| **Confirmation bias** | Seeking supporting material and ignoring disconfirming evidence | “Every silence confirms my suspicion.” |
| **Fundamental attribution error** | Overweighting disposition while overlooking situation | “They missed the train because they're lazy.” |

## 13. Crucial distinctions, safeguards and alternative explanations (14)

| Technique or reasoning error | What to notice | Fictional example |
|---|---|---|
| **Repair versus defensiveness** | A defensive reply is not proof of sustained abuse | “I panicked and denied it, but will correct the record.” |
| **Cooling-off pause versus punishment** | A time-limited communicated pause differs from silence used to control | “I'm overwhelmed; can we resume at seven?” |
| **Reasonable boundary versus control** | A limit on one's own participation differs from governing another adult | “I won't stay in a conversation with shouting.” |
| **Disagreement versus gaslighting** | A conflicting memory alone does not establish systematic reality manipulation | “I remember it differently; let's check.” |
| **Persuasion versus coercion** | Reasons and freely revisable choices differ from penalties for refusal | “Here are the bills; take a day to review.” |
| **Self-centred moment versus narcissistic disorder** | A single trait or exchange cannot establish a pervasive clinical disorder | “They dominated one conversation, not all their relationships.” |
| **Accidental fallacy versus strategic manipulation** | Bad reasoning can occur without a wish to exploit | “That analogy was weak; I'll reconsider.” |
| **Threat assessment versus motive guessing** | Risk can justify a boundary even when inner motive remains unknown | “I don't need to diagnose this to refuse monitoring.” |
| **Pattern versus isolated incident** | Frequency, power and response to objections change the interpretation | “One broken promise differs from repeated broken promises and penalties.” |
| **Context and vulnerability** | Economic, immigration or disability dependence can change the cost of refusing | “Saying no could threaten my housing.” |
| **Mutual escalation** | Both people may contribute to a conflict without equal power or equal harm | “Both shouted; only one controlled access to money.” |
| **Safety-first non-confrontation** | Recognising coercion does not always make direct confrontation safe | “I'll seek support and plan privately rather than debate.” |
| **Evidence humility** | Interpretations of intent should remain hypotheses | “I know what was said; I don't know what they meant.” |
| **Repair criterion** | Look for changed behaviour and restored choice, not only persuasive words | “The next month showed a different pattern.” |

## Recommended learning sequence

Begin with clear and observable behaviour: denial, blame shifting, gaslighting, DARVO, guilt pressure and boundary control. Continue into relationship, group and workplace contexts, then argument fallacies. Finish with ambiguous cases: legitimate disagreement, conflict escalation, cooling-off, imperfect memory and repair. Every module should contain counterexamples and encourage checking the actual facts before inferring motive.

**Review questions for every lesson:** What exactly was said? What would a reasonable alternative interpretation be? How did the other person respond when a clear question or boundary was raised? Is this recurring? What power difference matters? What response is safe and proportionate?
